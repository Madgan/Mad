## What the artefact is

The small, hand-made labelling system a shooter uses to keep track of memory cards during a job: a strip of coloured tape with a number written on it in marker, a card case in which the direction a card faces says whether it has been used, and a piece of tape that physically covers a card once its state has changed. It is the least glamorous paperwork in the trade and the one a solo wedding or event shooter performs dozens of times a day, usually in a hurry, because confusing a full card with an empty one loses the job.

The single image this would be, if it could be saved: one open, clear card case seen from directly above on a dark surface, holding four to six SD cards. Some cards sit label side up, some sit face down showing their blank backs. One card is wrapped in a band of green tape. One case lid carries a short strip of coloured paper tape with "A1" written on it in thick black marker.

## What the sources say (verified through search)

1. Orientation is the state. Before the shoot every card goes into the case face up. After a card has been shot it goes back into the same slot face down. The case is then readable at a glance: label visible means ready, blank back means used. (PremiumBeat)
2. A hidden mark. With single clear cases, the back of the case is marked with an X, so a used card, put back the other way round, sits behind the X. (PremiumBeat)
3. Tape that covers. For cards with no lock switch, a piece of gaff tape goes over the contacts after the card has been used. The card cannot be put into a camera without first removing the tape. (PremiumBeat)
4. The lock switch. On SD cards the write-protect switch is slid across after shooting. A physical position, not a colour. (PremiumBeat)
5. Every card is a numbered roll. Each new card is treated as a new roll with a short code such as A1 or A43 (camera letter plus running number), and a tag showing that code goes on the side of the camera while that card is in it. (The Black and Blue, 2010)
6. Green tape means checked. A card is wrapped in green tape only after its footage has been verified as backed up. Anyone may format a card that is wrapped in green. A card without the wrap is not formatted and goes back to be checked. The wrap has to be peeled off before the card can be used, so the release is a deliberate act. (The Black and Blue, Media Management 101)
7. Marker on the card, number or colour. In a wedding and event videography thread, shooters with several cameras label cards with a number or a colour, one writes straight onto the card with a marker because stickers curl and fall off, another uses a small label printer. (DVinfo.net)
8. The tape itself. Paper tape is the light, hand-tearable kind, most common in 1/2 inch and 1 inch widths; cloth tape is the stronger kind used on film magazines, where a colour is assigned per stock. Paper tape is sold in many flat colours including red, blue, white, black, grey, pink and purple; 1 inch cloth camera tape is sold in red, yellow, green, blue, white, black, grey and others. (The Black and Blue, 2011; AbelCine)

## Visual and motion properties

Marked [S] where it follows directly from the sources above, [G] where it is general knowledge of the object and was not checked against a picture here.

Layout and proportion
- [S] A case is a row or small grid of identical slots, each holding one card. All cards are the same size and shape, so the only things that vary from slot to slot are which way the card faces and what is stuck on it.
- [G] An SD card is 24 mm wide and 32 mm tall, a 3:4 portrait rectangle with one clipped corner at the top right of the label side. The label side carries print; the back is a plain dark face with a row of contacts at the top edge.
- [S] A tape label is a short strip, 1/2 inch or 1 inch wide. [G] It is usually torn by hand, so the two short ends are slightly ragged while the two long edges are factory straight. The strip is a wide landscape rectangle, roughly 1:3 to 1:4.

Colour
- [S] Flat, saturated, opaque tape colours on a dark object: green, red, yellow, blue, white. No gradients, no gloss.
- [G] Paper tape is matte with a very fine crepe texture. Marker ink on it is solid black with slightly soft edges.
- Colour is never the only carrier of state in this system: the green wrap also covers the card, the used card is also turned over, the gaff tape also blocks the contacts. [S]
- Starting values if the build takes the palette. These are estimates of common tape colours, not measured from an image, and must be re-checked against a real picture if one becomes available: tape green #1E9E4A, tape red #D4262C, tape yellow #F2C418, tape blue #1F5FBF, tape white #F3F1EA, marker black #111111.

Typography
- [S] The code is short: one letter and one or two digits (A1, A43). [G] It is written by hand in thick marker, in capitals, filling most of the height of the strip, usually a little off the horizontal. There is no other text on the strip.

How it sequences information (the part that matters for this build)
- [S] State is a physical position, and a change of state is a single physical move: turn the card over, slide the switch, wrap the tape, peel the tape. Each move takes one object from one state to another and leaves it at rest.
- [S] The sequence is strictly ordered and each step is visible afterwards: ready (face up), shot (face down), backed up and checked (green wrap), released (wrap peeled, card face up again). Nothing in the system is ambient or continuous. Every state can be read from a still photograph of the case.
- [S] Progress through a job is read across the row: how many cards are still face up against how many have been turned.
- [S] The identifier travels with the object. The same code is on the card and, while that card is in use, on the camera.

## Named elements the build may borrow (positive borrowings)

1. Flip to mark done. A step or record shown as a card-shaped tile that turns over on its vertical axis when it is completed, so that "done" is the other face of the same object and not a new colour on the old one. The reduced-motion state is simply the tile shown on its done face. Borrowed from: face up before, face down after.
2. The tape label. A short, flat-coloured strip with slightly ragged short ends, set a few degrees off the horizontal, carrying one short code in heavy capitals (for example a sample invoice or client code). Used as the page's way of naming an object, in place of a pill badge or an eyebrow line. Borrowed from: the 1 inch paper tape strip with A1 written in marker.
3. The row of slots as a progress readout. A fixed row of identical slots in which the count of turned tiles shows how far along the sequence is. No bar, no percentage. Borrowed from: the card case read at a glance.
4. Green wrap means checked. A band laid across an object only after a person has checked it, covering part of it so that the check is visible as an obstruction and not only as a colour. This maps directly onto a fact already on the live page (the AI reads old invoices and nothing is saved until the owner reviews and approves): the wrap can only go on after the review. Borrowed from: the green tape wrap on a backed-up card.
5. Tape palette. Green, red, yellow, blue and white as flat opaque fills on a dark ground, with marker black for the writing on them. Three or more chromatic colours are available from this anchor for the palette gate, with the caveat on the hex values above.
